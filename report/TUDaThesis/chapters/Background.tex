\section{Principles of Remote Physiological Sensing}
\label{sec:principles-rps}
%The theoretical foundation of your work rests on the ability to detect internal biological
%changes through external optical observations. Remote Photoplethysmography (rPPG):
% This thematic area explores how visible light interacts with the skin. As blood
% volume changes during the cardiac cycle, the absorption of specific wavelengths—particularly
% in the green spectrum—varies. This creates subtle color fluctuations in the skin
% that are detectable by standard imaging sensors. Thermal Infrared Sensing:
% Beyond reflected light, physiological states can be monitored via emitted heat.

Remote physiological sensing (RPS) integrates diverse non-invasive modalities
including optical, thermal, and radio-frequency (RF) sensors to monitor
biological indicators without physical contact \cite{hassanpour2025contactless}.
Within an academic framework, these signals are defined by their underlying
physiological mechanisms and the specialized extraction methodologies required
to isolate them from environmental noise. This section focuses on the sensing
paradigm and the acquisition of each signal class, whereas the mathematical
properties of the resulting waveforms and their implications for signal
reconstruction are characterized in Section \ref{sec:signal-characteristics}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heart Rate (HR) and Heart Rate Variability (HRV):} These
        signals reflect cardiac pulsatility and autonomic regulation. They are extracted
        via remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) from sub-visual skin color variations
        \cite{wang2017algorithmic}, microwave Doppler radar tracking of minute
        chest wall displacements \cite{islam2022radar}, or ballistocardiography (BCG)
        using ambient piezoelectric sensors to capture mechanical body recoil \cite{kjaer2026bcgfm}.
        Recent advancements utilize neuromorphic event cameras to capture high-frequency
        intensity transients induced by arterial pulsations with microsecond
        precision \cite{feng2026empd}.

    \item \textbf{Thermal Infrared (TIR) Sensing:} This modality monitors
        physiological states by detecting electromagnetic radiation emitted from
        the skin in the long-wave infrared spectrum ($8 \sim 14 \mu m$), enabling
        robust monitoring in complete darkness \cite{cho2019physiological}. TIR
        tracks Thermal Respiration (TR) by detecting periodic temperature
        fluctuations at the nostrils caused by the difference between inhaled
        and exhaled air \cite{cho2019physiological}. Additionally, it monitors
        autonomic stress through nasal temperature drops induced by
        vasoconstriction and quantifies sweat gland activation via the Pore
        Activation Index (PAI) \cite{cho2019physiological}.

    \item \textbf{Respiration and Tidal Volume:} Beyond thermal tracking, respiratory
        rate and tidal volume (air volume per cycle) are captured through microwave
        Doppler radar tracking of thoracic movement \cite{islam2022radar} and analyzing
        periodic intensity changes in RGB video sequences \cite{hassanpour2025contactless}.

    \item \textbf{Blood Oxygen Saturation ($\text{SpO}^{2}$):} This signal
        measures the percentage of oxygenated hemoglobin. Contactless estimation
        utilizes the ``Ratio of Ratios'' principle, analyzing the differential
        absorption of specific wavelengths, typically red and blue or infrared,
        captured by RGB cameras and processed through spatial-temporal maps (STMaps)
        \cite{cheng2024contactless}.

    \item \textbf{Blood Pressure (BP):} RPS reconstructs blood pressure by fusing
        multimodal features, such as rPPG waveforms and thermal signatures, through
        cross-modal deep learning architectures that can simultaneously perceive
        frame-level pulse variations \cite{hassanpour2025contactless, cen2026robust}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Spectral and Geometric Multimodality}
Spectral and Geometric Multimodality in remote physiological sensing (RPS)
refers to the synergistic integration of radiometric information such as RGB and
thermal sequences with spatial structural data such as 3D facial meshes to
mitigate sensing ambiguities induced by subject movement and environmental
variability \cite{hassanpour2025contactless}. This approach addresses the limitations
of single-modality systems through two primary mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radiometric Texture vs. 3D Geometry:} While 2D spectral
        modalities provide high-fidelity color-based physiological indicators
        like blood volume pulse (BVP) variations
        \cite{wang2017algorithmic, cen2026robust}, they are inherently sensitive
        to motion-induced photometric noise and perspective distortion \cite{cen2026robust,
        islam2022radar}. The incorporation of 3D facial geometry captured via
        mesh tracking and Euler angles enables active head pose compensation and
        expression-invariant monitoring. By mapping spectral textures onto dynamic
        3D models, systems can maintain signal integrity during rotation or non-rigid
        deformations that would otherwise mask the subtle chromatic fluctuations
        required for rPPG extraction.

    \item \textbf{Landmark-Based ROI Indexing:} Effective RPS requires precise
        localization of Regions of Interest (ROIs), as physiological signatures are
        not uniformly distributed across the facial surface \cite{cheng2024contactless,
        cho2019physiological}. Point-based tracking frameworks utilize
        standardized landmark indices, such as the 49-point 2D, 83-point 3D, and
        28-point thermal sets utilized in the BP4D+ dataset corpus \cite{zhang2014bp4d},
        to define and track these regions across multimodal domains. This cross-modal indexing
        is critical for consistent signal extraction from areas with high micro-vascular
        density, such as the forehead and cheeks, or high thermal contrast, such
        as the perinasal regions for respiratory monitoring.
\end{itemize}

\section{Temporal Heterogeneity and Signal Alignment}
Temporal heterogeneity in remote physiological sensing arises from the
fundamental Sampling Rate Discrepancy between visual acquisition systems and
clinical-grade physiological sensors. This discrepancy creates a significant barrier
to the reconstruction of high-fidelity waveforms from sparse visual data:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Video-Signal Duality:} Non-invasive monitoring frameworks
        typically ingest visual data at standard frame rates (e.g., $25 \sim 30$
        fps), whereas ground-truth physiological signals—such as Electrocardiography
        (ECG) or Blood Volume Pulse (BVP)—are often sampled at frequencies as
        high as 1000 Hz to capture fine-grained morphology
        \cite{feghoul2026deep}. This duality necessitates specialized synchronization
        frameworks to eliminate temporal drift between heterogeneous sensors and
        ensure precise alignment between the asynchronous visual stream and the continuous
        physiological waveform \cite{feng2026empd}.

    \item \textbf{Temporal Resolution and Under-Sampling:} Traditional
        frame-based sensors are limited by exposure-induced motion blur and a fixed
        temporal resolution that often under-samples high-frequency cardiovascular
        events \cite{feng2026empd, cheng2026fcus}. To bridge this gap, research
        utilizes the quasi-periodic nature of biological waveforms, employing methodologies
        like temporal binning to aggregate signals into fixed intervals or downsampling
        the high-frequency ground truth to match the video frame rate for model training
        \cite{feghoul2026deep}. Advanced neuromorphic approaches,
        such as event-based vision, offer a potential solution by providing microsecond-level
        timestamp precision, effectively capturing the ultra-fast physiological
        transients that are lost in low-frequency 2D video.
\end{itemize}

\section{Characteristics of Physiological Targets}
\label{sec:signal-characteristics}
While Section \ref{sec:principles-rps} addressed how the target signals are
acquired, the signals themselves are heterogeneous in their mathematical
properties, and a precise characterization of these properties is a prerequisite
for signal reconstruction. The choice of representation, sampling strategy, and
modeling assumptions must reflect the underlying signal structure: depending on
their physiological origin, the target signals may be quasi-periodic,
continuous, or sporadic, and they are quantified in distinct physical units that
encode different aspects of autonomic and circulatory activity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular and Respiratory Signals:} Heart rate and
        respiration are quasi-periodic signals, measured in beats or breaths
        per minute. Their periodicity constitutes a strong prior that
        reconstruction models can exploit to disambiguate the physiological
        component from noise and motion artifacts.

    \item \textbf{Hemodynamic Signals:} Blood pressure---comprising systolic,
        diastolic, and mean values---represents continuous pressure variations
        within the circulatory system, typically measured in millimeters of
        mercury (mmHg). In contrast to periodic cardiac signals, blood pressure
        exhibits a slower, more sustained dynamic that arises from vascular tone
        and peripheral resistance, requiring models that capture long-range
        temporal dependencies.

    \item \textbf{Electrodermal Activity (EDA):} Unlike periodic cardiac signals,
        EDA measures skin conductance in microsiemens and reflects sympathetic
        nervous system arousal. It is characterized by more sporadic, tonic, and
        phasic changes, which makes its reconstruction particularly challenging:
        models must capture transient, event-driven dynamics rather than steady
        rhythmic patterns, and must contend with a signal that is both slowly
        drifting and punctuated by rapid responses.
\end{itemize}

\section{Affective-Physiological Coupling}
Affective-physiological coupling describes the systematic relationship between a
subject's emotional state and the accompanying physiological responses. In
contrast to controlled, instructed experimental settings, spontaneous conditions
do not impose a fixed behavioral protocol: subjects move naturally, express
emotions freely, and interact with their environment without restriction. As a
consequence, the recorded physiological signals are superimposed upon a
continuously varying baseline that reflects both internal affective state and
external behavior. Establishing this coupling is essential for signal
reconstruction, since the target waveforms must be recovered from recordings in
which the physiological component of interest is entangled with unconstrained
affective and motor activity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Autonomic Response to Stimuli:} Physiological signals are not
        static but fluctuate with internal emotional states, ranging from
        amusement and surprise to physical pain and disgust. These fluctuations
        are mediated by the autonomic nervous system, which modulates
        cardiovascular, respiratory, and electrodermal activity according to the
        level of affective arousal. For signal reconstruction, this implies that
        the target signal is embedded in a state-dependent baseline that varies
        across subjects and over time; reconstruction models must therefore
        remain robust to such non-stationarity rather than assuming a fixed,
        stationary waveform.

    \item \textbf{Motion and Occlusion:} In a spontaneous environment, subjects
        are prone to head movements, facial expressions, and partial occlusions
        of the regions of interest. These behaviors introduce motion-induced
        photometric noise and temporarily obscure the facial areas from which
        physiological information is extracted. Signal reconstruction models
        must consequently remain reliable when facial regions are partially out
        of view or distorted by intense expressions, motivating the use of
        spatial redundancy and temporal continuity to compensate for missing or
        corrupted observations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Vision Transformers (ViT)}
Vision Transformers have become a central building block in modern computer
vision, and they are particularly relevant to the signal reconstruction tasks
considered in this thesis. Remote physiological signals are subtle, spatially
distributed, and strongly periodic, so their recovery benefits from a model that
integrates global context across the entire face rather than relying on local
receptive fields alone. By tokenizing visual input into discrete patches and
processing them with self-attention, a Vision Transformer captures long-range
spatial dependencies, while its self-supervised pre-training regime---exemplified
by the Masked Autoencoder---allows it to be trained on large amounts of
unlabeled video before being adapted to a specific downstream task. This section
introduces the Vision Transformer architecture, its attention mechanism, and the
Masked Autoencoder paradigm that forms the methodological foundation of this
thesis.

\subsection{Image Patching and Tokenization}
The Vision Transformer (ViT) \cite{dosovitskiy2021image} marked a departure from
convolutional architectures by adapting the Transformer, originally developed
for natural language processing, to visual data. Instead of relying on local
pixel neighborhoods, a ViT partitions an input image into a sequence of regular,
non-overlapping patches, such as $16 \times 16$ pixels. Each patch is flattened
and mapped through a learned linear projection into a latent vector space of
dimension $D$, effectively treating every image segment as a ``visual token'' in
direct analogy to a word embedding in BERT \cite{devlin-etal-2019-bert}. An
additional learnable class token is prepended to the sequence and serves as an
aggregate representation of the entire image, while learnable positional
embeddings are added to the patch tokens so that the model retains information
about the spatial layout of the patches. Because self-attention is
permutation-invariant, these positional embeddings are essential: without them,
the encoder could not distinguish the order in which patches occur.

\subsection{Self-Attention Mechanism}
The core mechanism of the ViT is multi-head self-attention
\cite{vaswani2017attention}, which enables the model to capture global
dependencies across the entire image simultaneously. Whereas convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) rely on local receptive fields and hierarchical feature
extraction, a ViT allows every visual token to interact with every other token,
providing a comprehensive spatial context and substantially reducing the
inductive biases---such as translation invariance and locality---inherent in
CNNs. The resulting encoder consists of a stack of transformer blocks, each
comprising a multi-head self-attention layer and a multi-layer perceptron (MLP),
interleaved with layer normalization and residual connections. This architecture
has proven highly scalable, supporting very large models trained on large-scale
self-supervised pre-training tasks \cite{he2022masked} while learning complex
spatial relationships from data rather than from hand-crafted priors.

\subsection{Masked Autoencoders (MAE)}
The foundational paradigm for this research is the Masked Autoencoder (MAE)
\cite{he2022masked}, a self-supervised approach that applies the logic of masked
language modeling to visual data. MAE employs an asymmetric encoder-decoder
architecture in which a large fraction of the image patches (typically 75\%) is
randomly masked out:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The encoder processes only the small subset of visible (unmasked)
        image patches, drastically reducing memory consumption and computational
        overhead while forcing the model to work from highly incomplete input.

    \item The decoder is significantly smaller and is used only during the
        pre-training phase to reconstruct the masked patches from the latent
        representations provided by the encoder.
\end{itemize}
Because the heavy encoder never sees the mask tokens during pre-training, it
cannot fall back on the trivial shortcut of copying the masked input and must
instead learn a semantically meaningful latent representation of the visible
patches. The lightweight decoder then maps this rich latent back to the
reconstructed image, measuring the reconstruction error on the masked regions.
After pre-training is completed, the decoder is discarded and the encoder is
fine-tuned for downstream tasks using the full set of tokens. This design is
directly aligned with the signal reconstruction problem studied in this thesis:
just as MAE reconstructs masked patches from their visible context, the models
developed here reconstruct physiological waveforms from partially observable and
noise-corrupted visual data.
