\section{Evolution of Remote Physiological Sensing}
The field of remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) has transitioned from handcrafted
mathematical models to deep neural architectures. Early supervised benchmarks
like PhysNet and PhysFormer established the capability to extract vital signs
from facial video \cite{savic2024physunet}. However, these models often lack
interpretability and robustness under varying environmental conditions. To address
this, PHASE-Net introduces a physics-informed approach, grounding its attention mechanism
in the Navier–Stokes equations to model pulse dynamics as second-order damped harmonic
oscillators \cite{zhao2024phasenet}. Furthermore, to overcome the temporal sampling
limitations of frame-based sensors (typically $30 \sim 60$ fps), recent work
with the EMPD dataset has introduced event-based neuromorphic sensing, which offers
microsecond-level resolution to bridge the "sampling gap" at the hardware level \cite{feng2026empd}.

\section{Foundations of Masked Autoencoders (MAE) in Spatiotemporal Data}
The success of self-supervised learning (SSL) in natural language processing has
been effectively ported to the visual domain via Masked Autoencoders. VideoMAE
demonstrated that because of the high temporal redundancy in video data, extremely
high masking ratios (90\% to 95\%) are not only viable but superior for forcing a
model to learn high-level spatiotemporal structures \cite{tong2022videomae}.
This framework was further scaled by VideoMAE V2, which introduced a "dual
masking" strategy—masking both the encoder and the decoder—to significantly reduce
computational overhead for billion-parameter models \cite{wang2023videomaev2}.
For multi-sensor environments, MV2MAE extended this paradigm to synchronized multi-view
videos, utilizing a cross-view reconstruction task to inject geometric
information and ensure representation robustness to viewpoint shifts
\cite{shah2023mv2mae}.

\section{Multimodal Scaffolding and Joint Embedding Spaces}
Your research sits at the intersection of diverse modalities (RGB, 3D, and Thermal).
A critical advancement in this space is MultiMAE, which shows that pre-training
a single Transformer on multiple dense visual tasks (e.g., RGB, depth, and semantic
segmentation) creates a generalist model that can handle unpredicted modality
dropout at inference \cite{bachmann2022multimae}. Furthermore, ImageBind
provides a framework for learning joint embeddings across six disparate
modalities—including thermal and IMU—by using images as a "binding" modality to
align signals that are rarely observed together \cite{girdhar2023imagebind}. Moving
beyond symmetric alignment, xMAE reframes the problem as one of "asymmetric temporal
observability," using one signal (e.g., PPG) as training-time scaffolding to reconstruct
a directional second signal (e.g., ECG), thereby capturing the electrical-to-mechanical
delay inherent in human physiology \cite{zhou2026xmae}.

\section{Multiscale Spectral and Physiological Modeling}

Because physiological signals are non-stationary and quasi-periodic, standard
time-domain masking is often insufficient. bioFAME addresses this by introducing
frequency-aware Transformers that parameterize representations in the frequency space,
ensuring robustness to modality mismatch in wearable data \cite{liu2024biofame}.
The MAEConformer architecture combines convolutional local feature extraction with
Transformer-based long-range attention, employing a Multi-Resolution STFT (MR-STFT)
loss to jointly learn temporal and spectral characteristics
\cite{yu2026maeconformer}. To explicitly model the hierarchical rhythms of biosignals,
the MMR (Wavelet-Driven Masked Multiscale Reconstruction) framework utilizes the
Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) \cite{thukral2026wavelet}. By reconstructing masked
wavelet coefficients, MMR captures features ranging from fine-grained waveform
morphology to global rhythmic dynamics, providing a robust foundation for downstream
health monitoring \cite{thukral2026wavelet}. Finally, rPPG-MAE adapts these SSL paradigms
specifically for remote measurement by leveraging the inherent self-similar priors
found in biological pulse signals \cite{liu2023rppgmae}.

\section{Analysis of Related Work}

\subsection{Research Gaps}

The existing literature demonstrates that Masked Autoencoders (MAEs) are highly effective
at learning spatiotemporal representations for both visual \cite{tong2022videomae,
wang2023videomaev2} and physiological data \cite{liu2023rppgmae, savic2024physunet}.
However, current state-of-the-art (SOTA) approaches are primarily designed for representation
learning—where the reconstruction task is a pretext to improve downstream classification
or regression accuracy—rather than for the explicit generative recovery of a missing
sensor stream. There are three critical gaps in the current research landscape
that prevent existing models from addressing your main Research Question (RQ)
regarding targeted sensor failure and information loss:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Downstream vs.\ Generative Objective:} Most physiological
        MAEs, such as MAEConformer \cite{yu2026maeconformer} and bioFAME \cite{liu2024biofame},
        evaluate success through performance on specific medical tasks like HIE
        severity classification or cardiovascular outcome prediction. While xMAE
        \cite{zhou2026xmae} utilizes cross-modal reconstruction as a ``scaffolding''
        task, its primary goal is to capture directional inductive biases to
        enhance the PPG representation, not to quantify the fidelity of the reconstructed
        signal for its own sake. There is a lack of research that treats the
        generative reconstruction of the raw signal as the primary metric to
        determine how much biological information (e.g., HRV morphology,
        hemodynamic transients) can be recovered when a physical sensor fails.

    \item \textbf{Robustness vs.\ Active Reconstruction during Failure:} Current
        multimodal frameworks like MultiMAE \cite{bachmann2022multimae} and
        ImageBind \cite{girdhar2023imagebind} focus on ``binding'' disparate
        modalities into a joint embedding space to achieve robustness against
        unpredicted modality dropout. While these models can function if a sensor
        fails, they are not optimized for cross-modal predictive coding---specifically
        the act of using surviving modalities (e.g., 2D RGB and 3D geometry) to
        actively ``fill in'' the missing high-dimensional sensor stream (e.g., Thermal
        Infrared). Existing work handles ``missingness'' as a noise-insensitivity
        problem, whereas your RQ frames it as a structured inference problem.

    \item \textbf{Lack of Information Loss Quantification in the Signal Domain:}
        The research community currently lacks a standardized framework for
        measuring information loss during sensor failure in non-invasive sensing.
        While MMR (Wavelet-Driven Masked Multiscale Reconstruction) \cite{thukral2026wavelet}
        and MAEConformer \cite{yu2026maeconformer} utilize spectral losses (DWT
        and STFT) to improve reconstruction, they do not quantify how much ``physiological
        signal information'' is systematically discarded when forced to predict
        a modality across spectral gaps (e.g., reconstructing thermal heat signatures
        from RGB color changes). Existing metrics focus on error (MAE/RMSE) or correlation
        \cite{savic2024physunet}, but they do not address the entropy or
        physiological completeness of the reconstructed stream during a targeted
        hardware failure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Summary}
While the community has explored within-modality interpolation and multi-modal task
performance, there is a distinct gap in understanding cross-modal predictive
limits. Your thesis fills this gap by shifting the focus from "representations
for classification" to "predictive coding for sensor recovery." By utilizing the
BP4D+ environment to simulate targeted sensor failures, your work will provide
the first rigorous analysis of the extent to which the cross-modal redundancy of
human physiology allows a deep model to compensate for missing hardware.
