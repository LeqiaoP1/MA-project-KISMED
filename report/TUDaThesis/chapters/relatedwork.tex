\chapter{Related Work}
\label{ch:relatedwork}
%*****************************************
This chapter gives a comprehensive overview of the related work by other authors
and analyzes why the existing approaches are not capable of solving the problem
defined in the introduction, thereby defining the research gap. The review is
organized along four complementary dimensions of research that jointly span the
design space of the thesis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Modality Dimension: \textbf{Remote physiological sensing} (Section~\ref{sec:rw-sensing})
    traces the evolution of remote physiological sensing across RGB, thermal
    infrared, and 3D geometry and establishes the modalities that the thesis
    seeks to fuse.
    \item The Architectural Dimension: \textbf{Masked Autoencoders for spatiotemporal data}
    (Section~\ref{sec:rw-mae}) reviews Masked Autoencoders (MAEs), the
    architectural paradigm underlying the proposed approach.
    \item The Fusion Dimension: \textbf{Multimodal learning and joint embedding spaces}
    (Section~\ref{sec:rw-multimodal}) examines how information from
    heterogeneous sensors can be shared across modalities.
    \item The Optimization Dimension: \textbf{Signal-aware reconstruction objectives} (Section~\ref{sec:rw-spectral})
    complements this discussion with techniques that explicitly exploit the
    structure of physiological signals, from spectral losses to self-similarity
    priors.
\end{itemize}
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:rw-analysis} synthesizes these threads and defines
the research gap addressed by this thesis.

\section{Evolution of Remote Physiological Sensing}
\label{sec:rw-sensing}
The field of remote photoplethysmography (rPPG) has evolved from handcrafted
mathematical models to deep neural architectures. Most existing deep learning models act as "black boxes".
Early supervised models such as PhySU-Net~\cite{savic2024physunet} and PhysFormer~\cite{yu2022physformerfacialvideobasedphysiological} 
demonstrate that vital signs can be accurately reconstructed from facial videos of the dataset VIPL-HR~\cite{niu2018viplhrmultimodaldatabasepulse}.
by utilizing a long-temporal context Transformer that operates on intermediate spatital temporal representations. It help see through the 
noise rather than teching it the underlying physiological dynamics.

These heuristic models often lack interpretability and degrade under varying environmental conditions. 
To address these limitations, PHASE-Net~\cite{zhao2025phasenet} introduces a physics-informed paradigm which describes how blood fluids through
the body like a second-order damped harmonic oscillators. 
The authors provide a theoretical justification for utilizing a causal Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) as the core dynamics modeling block.
It forces the model to learn the underlying physiological dynamics rather than just fitting the data.

The modalities relevant to this thesis extend beyond visible-spectrum rPPG. Two
extra ``senses'' are considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thermal infrared (TIR) imaging} captures the long-wave
    infrared radiation ($8$--$14\,\mu m$) emitted by the skin and is therefore
    invariant to visible illumination; it enables thermal respiration
    monitoring at the nostrils, autonomic-stress assessment through nasal
    temperature drops, and the quantification of sweat-gland activation
    \cite{cho2019physiological}. Because these signatures are driven by the
    same autonomic processes that modulate the cardiovascular signal, TIR
    constitutes a physiologically redundant yet spectrally disjoint companion
    to RGB.
    \item \textbf{3D facial geometry}: point-based tracking frameworks register
    spectral textures onto dynamic 3D facial meshes and use standardized
    landmark sets---such as the 49-point 2D, 83-point 3D, and 28-point thermal
    landmarks of the BP4D+ corpus \cite{zhang2014bp4d}---to define and track
    regions of interest across modalities \cite{hassanpour2025contactless}.
    This geometric anchoring compensates for head motion and perspective
    distortion that would otherwise mask the subtle chromatic fluctuations
    exploited by rPPG \cite{cheng2024contactless}.
\end{itemize}

These evolved algorithmic modeling with additional modalities assumes that the underlying sensor is functioning. 
None of them can compensate for a sensor that has failed completely. These limitations
correspond exactly to the scenario investigated in this thesis and highlight the
need for \emph{cross-modal} redundancy rather than sensor-level redundancy alone.

\section{Masked Autoencoders for Spatiotemporal Data}
\label{sec:rw-mae}
The success of self-supervised learning (SSL) in natural language processing has
been ported to the visual domain through Masked Autoencoders (MAEs)
\cite{he2022masked}. Whereas the original MAE randomly masks a large fraction of
spatial patches in still images, VideoMAE extends this paradigm to video and
shows that the high temporal redundancy of video data makes extremely high
masking ratios ($90\%$--$95\%$) not only viable but beneficial: aggressive
masking forces the model to reason about high-level spatiotemporal structure
rather than relying on local temporal smoothness \cite{tong2022videomae}. 
VideoMAE masks entire \emph{spatiotemporal tubes} that span the temporal dimension, 
so that the reconstruction task cannot be solved by copying information across adjacent frames. 
This tube-masking strategy is directly relevant to the temporal masking investigated in Sub-RQ~2 of this thesis. 

VideoMAE V2 later scales this framework and introduces a dual-masking strategy that masks both encoder
and decoder inputs, substantially reducing the computational overhead of billion-parameter models \cite{wang2023videomaev2}. 

For multi-sensor environments, MV2MAE extends the MAE paradigm to synchronized multi-view videos,
using a cross-view reconstruction task to inject geometric information and to
ensure representation robustness to viewpoint shifts \cite{shah2024mv2mae}.

The common thread of these works is that the input and target modalities
coincide: the model reconstructs the same stream from which the visible context
was drawn. They therefore establish strong within-modality priors, but leave the
question of \emph{cross-modal} reconstruction--recovering a completely missing
stream from the remaining sensors--largely unaddressed. Moreover, tube-masking
studies have so far targeted generic video rather than physiological signals
recovered from noisy, multimodal video, leaving the quantitative impact of
extreme masking ratios on physiological reconstruction (Sub-RQ~2) unexplored.

Recovering missing visual content is not exclusive to masked reconstruction;
explicit generative frameworks, such as conditional image-to-image translation
\cite{isola2017pix2pix} and video inpainting, synthesize the absent stream
directly. These methods, however, require paired targets for supervised training
and are typically optimized for perceptual fidelity rather than for the recovery
of a physiologically meaningful signal, and they do not double as a
self-supervised pre-training objective. The masked-reconstruction paradigm
adopted in this thesis instead unifies the pre-training task with the recovery
objective, which is why it is the basis of the proposed approach.

\section{Multimodal Learning and Joint Embedding Spaces}
\label{sec:rw-multimodal}
This thesis is situated at the fusion of heterogeneous modalities (RGB, 3D
geometry, thermal infrared and pyhisiological signals), so multimodal learning frameworks are of
central relevance. MultiMAE shows that pre-training a single Transformer on
multiple dense visual tasks (e.g., RGB, depth, and semantic segmentation) yields
a generalist model that remains robust to unpredicted modality dropout at
inference time \cite{bachmann2022multimae}. 

ImageBind provides a complementary framework for learning joint embeddings across six disparate
modalities---including thermal and IMU---by using images as a \enquote{binding}
modality that aligns signals that are rarely observed together
\cite{girdhar2023imagebind}. 

Moving beyond symmetric alignment, xMAE reframes cross-modal learning as a problem of \emph{asymmetric temporal observability}:
one signal (e.g., PPG) is used as training-time scaffolding to reconstruct a
directionally dependent second signal (e.g., ECG), thereby capturing the
electrical-to-mechanical delay inherent in human physiology \cite{zhou2026xmae}.

These frameworks establish that shared embedding spaces can align physiologically related modalities and 
that a model can tolerate the absence of one modality during inference. 
However, they treat missingness as a robustness problem: the model is expected to produce a \emph{usable representation} despite
dropout, not to \emph{generate a faithful reconstruction} of the missing raw
stream. The fidelity of the recovered signal is therefore neither optimized nor
quantified, which lies at the heart of the main research question (RQ) of this
thesis.

\section{Signal-Aware Reconstruction Objectives for Physiological Signals}
\label{sec:rw-spectral}
Because physiological signals are non-stationary and quasi-periodic, standard
time-domain masking is often insufficient. bioFAME~\cite{liu2024biofame} addresses this by introducing
frequency-aware Transformers that parameterize representations in the frequency
domain, ensuring robustness to modality mismatch in wearable data. 

The MAEConformer~\cite{yu2026maeconformer} architecture combines convolutional
local feature extraction with Transformer-based long-range attention and employs
a Multi-Resolution STFT (MR-STFT) loss to learn temporal and spectral
characteristics jointly. 

To model the hierarchical rhythms of biosignals explicitly, the MMR (Wavelet-Driven Masked Multiscale
Reconstruction)~\cite{thukral2026wavelet} framework applies the Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT).
By reconstructing masked wavelet coefficients, MMR captures features ranging from
fine-grained waveform morphology to global rhythmic dynamics, providing a robust
foundation for downstream health monitoring.

In a complementary line of work, rPPG-MAE~\cite{liu2023rppgmae} adapts these self-supervised paradigms
to remote measurement by exploiting the self-similar priors inherent in
biological pulse signals rather than an explicit spectral loss.

Together, these methods refine the reconstruction objective with spectral or
structure-aware losses and show that exploiting the quasi-periodic structure of
physiological signals improves reconstruction fidelity. Nevertheless, they
operate almost exclusively within a single modality and on wearable or
contact-based signals.

\section{Analysis of Related Work}
\label{sec:rw-analysis}
The existing literature demonstrates that MAEs are highly effective at learning
spatiotemporal representations for both visual \cite{tong2022videomae,
wang2023videomaev2} and physiological data \cite{liu2023rppgmae, savic2024physunet},
and that multimodal joint embedding spaces can align heterogeneous physiological
modalities \cite{bachmann2022multimae, girdhar2023imagebind, zhou2026xmae}.
However, current state-of-the-art approaches are predominantly designed for
representation learning---in which the reconstruction task serves as a pretext
to improve downstream classification or regression accuracy---rather than for
the explicit generative recovery of a missing sensor stream. This leaves three
critical gaps that prevent existing models from addressing the main research
question (RQ) regarding targeted sensor failure and information loss:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Downstream vs.\ Generative Objective (RQ):} Most
        physiological MAEs, such as MAEConformer \cite{yu2026maeconformer} and
        bioFAME \cite{liu2024biofame}, evaluate success by performance on
        specific medical tasks, for example HIE severity classification or
        cardiovascular outcome prediction. While xMAE \cite{zhou2026xmae} uses
        cross-modal reconstruction as a scaffolding task, its primary goal is to
        capture directional inductive biases that enhance the PPG representation,
        not to quantify the fidelity of the reconstructed signal for its own
        sake. Consequently, no method treats the generative reconstruction of
        the raw signal as the primary metric, i.e., quantifying how much
        biological information (e.g., HRV morphology, hemodynamic transients)
        can be recovered when a physical sensor fails.

    \item \textbf{Robustness vs.\ Active Reconstruction during Failure (RQ, Sub-RQ~1):}
        Multimodal frameworks such as MultiMAE \cite{bachmann2022multimae} and
        ImageBind \cite{girdhar2023imagebind} bind disparate modalities into a
        joint embedding space to remain functional under unpredicted modality
        dropout, but they are not optimized for cross-modal predictive
        coding---the use of surviving modalities (e.g., RGB video and 3D
        geometry) to actively reconstruct a missing high-dimensional sensor
        stream (e.g., thermal infrared). Existing work thus treats missingness
        as a noise-insensitivity problem, whereas the RQ of this thesis frames
        it as a structured inference problem solved by a single unified encoder
        capable of fusing 1D time series with 2D video (Sub-RQ~1).

    \item \textbf{Lack of Information-Loss Quantification in the Signal Domain (RQ):}
        MMR \cite{thukral2026wavelet} and MAEConformer \cite{yu2026maeconformer}
        use spectral losses (DWT and STFT) to improve reconstruction, but they do
        not quantify how much physiological signal information is systematically
        discarded when a modality must be predicted across a spectral gap (e.g.,
        reconstructing thermal heat signatures from RGB color changes). Existing
        metrics focus on error (MAE/RMSE) or correlation \cite{savic2024physunet},
        not on the entropy or physiological completeness of the reconstructed
        stream during a targeted hardware failure.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Summary and Research Gap}
Taken together, no reviewed method combines a unified encoder for 1D time series
and 2D video with an explicit, measurable generative recovery of a failed
sensor. This thesis closes this gap by shifting the focus from
\enquote{representations for classification} to \enquote{predictive coding for
sensor recovery}. Concretely, the thesis contributes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a unified Vision Transformer encoder that fuses 1D physiological
    time series (e.g., EDA, heart rate) with 2D RGB and thermal video
    (Sub-RQ~1);
    \item a systematic study of temporal tube masking and extreme masking
    ratios ($90\%$ and above) for the reconstruction of physiological
    indicators from noisy video (Sub-RQ~2); and
    \item the first, to our knowledge, rigorous analysis, based on simulated
    sensor failures in the BP4D+ corpus \cite{zhang2014bp4d}, which synchronizes
    RGB, thermal, and 3D facial sequences with physiological signals, of the
    extent to which the cross-modal redundancy of human physiology enables a
    deep model to compensate for missing hardware.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:rw-overview} condenses the reviewed literature along four
dimensions that are decisive for the problem of this thesis: the modality of the
input, the reconstruction objective, whether the reconstruction target lies
within the same modality, across viewpoints, or across modalities, and whether a
completely missing sensor stream is explicitly handled. Three tiers emerge.
First, the majority of methods operate \emph{within} a single modality---reconstructing
masked parts of the same stream, be it video (VideoMAE) or physiological signal
(bioFAME, MAEConformer, MMR, rPPG-MAE)---and therefore cannot recover a stream
that is entirely absent. Second, MV2MAE reconstructs one viewpoint from another
within the video modality; although the target view differs from the source, the
underlying modality is unchanged, so it likewise cannot recover a stream whose
sensor has failed outright. Third, the methods that do operate \emph{across}
modalities (MultiMAE, ImageBind, xMAE) treat missingness either as a robustness
requirement during pre-training or as a scaffolding objective for representation
learning; none of them optimizes or quantifies the generative fidelity of a
reconstructed sensor stream.
